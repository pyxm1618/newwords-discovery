# SEO Opportunity Filter

## Core question

For every rising term, ask:

> **Has this change created a search need that can become a durable SEO asset, tool, product, or long-lived page?**

The target is not “hot terms.” The target is **new or renewed search demand whose supply can be built and accumulated over time**.

Industry is not a filter. AI, games, entertainment, travel, consumer goods, finance, education, home improvement, hobbies, B2B, and any other domain can qualify. Judge the demand structure, not the category.

## Decision sequence

### 1. Identify the real user job

Determine why people are searching now and what they are trying to accomplish.

Strong opportunity signals are repeatable jobs such as:

- calculate, convert, estimate, check, compare;
- generate, create, plan, design, customize;
- find, track, monitor, look up, browse;
- learn, apply, configure, fix, choose;
- use a new product, service, standard, workflow, role, or concept.

A query whose main job is merely “what just happened?” is usually event consumption rather than a durable SEO need.

### 2. Apply the asset test

Ask whether the need can be served repeatedly by something that compounds as an SEO asset.

Possible forms include, but are not limited to:

- tool or calculator;
- checker, converter, tracker, or lookup;
- database, directory, catalog, or structured reference;
- guide, tutorial, knowledge base, or resource hub;
- template, planner, worksheet, or generator;
- comparison or selection experience;
- vertical product, workflow, SaaS, community, or interactive content.

Do **not** keep a term merely because an agent can imagine a product. The user need itself must be supported by evidence.

### 3. Apply the durability test

Ask whether users will continue to encounter this job after the immediate trigger fades.

Durability does not mean permanent. A useful opportunity may last weeks, months, or years. What matters is that new users can repeatedly arrive with the same underlying job.

Evidence can include:

- continued or repeated interest in Trends history;
- a new concept/product/service that persists after launch;
- recurring user questions around the same task;
- a growing ecosystem of related searches;
- a changed workflow, behavior, standard, or market structure.

### 4. Treat events as triggers, not opportunities

**The raw event itself is not a KEEP.**

Typical raw-event terms that usually become `DROP`:

- breaking news;
- sports match/result;
- celebrity appearance, relationship, death, or controversy;
- film/TV release or episode title;
- disaster/accident;
- short promotion or sale;
- one-off company/legal incident;
- one-time announcement whose search intent is only informational.

Before discarding the whole lead, inspect whether the event has created a new derivative user job.

Examples of valid event-derived patterns can come from any domain:

- a new travel authorization creates an eligibility/checklist need;
- a new consumer device category creates compatibility or sizing needs;
- a new tariff or pricing structure creates comparison/calculation needs;
- a new standard or workflow creates migration/configuration needs;
- a new cultural behavior creates planning/template/service needs.

These are examples, not an allowed-category list.

### 5. Do not invent derivative keywords

A derivative-demand candidate must have real evidence.

Acceptable evidence can include:

- exact query phrasing visible in search results or related-search surfaces;
- multiple recent pages/questions using the same task phrasing;
- real autocomplete/keyword-tool evidence;
- measurable keyword data from the repository API or another verified source.

If the need is plausible but the query wording is not evidenced, keep it as a hypothesis under `WATCH`; do not promote invented wording to `KEEP`.

## Opportunity types

### TRUE_NEW

Use when the underlying search need is genuinely new or newly emerging.

Typical history shape:

- earlier history absent, sparse, or low;
- recent appearance is materially different from the historical baseline;
- the need is forming rather than merely repeating an old seasonal/event pattern.

Do not require a fixed numeric threshold unless validated later. Interpret the curve and evidence together.

### RESURGENCE

Use when an older term or need has a **new reason to matter again** and the renewed demand can produce durable search intent.

A simple publicity spike is not enough.

Keep only when the current rise reflects a changed use case, workflow, audience, market, product category, or other repeatable user need.

### EVENT_DERIVED

Use when an event triggers a **new, repeatable user task**.

The event term itself can remain `DROP`; the derivative term may be `KEEP`.

The derivative need must survive the event as a useful task and must be evidence-backed.

## WATCH

Use `WATCH` when the opportunity may be real but evidence is incomplete.

Common reasons:

- term/entity meaning is ambiguous;
- too little history exists to distinguish a pulse from demand formation;
- the derivative job appears plausible but exact query evidence is missing;
- why-now evidence is conflicting;
- demand may be durable but the current rise is too event-bound to decide.

State the exact unresolved question. Do not use `WATCH` as a vague middle bucket.

## DROP

Use `DROP` when the available evidence indicates no durable SEO opportunity.

Common reasons:

- pure event/news consumption;
- recurring seasonal or match-day demand with no new job;
- celebrity/entertainment interest with no derivative task;
- promotion-only intent;
- a mature old term merely receiving attention again;
- no repeatable user job;
- no credible asset thesis supported by real demand.

A `DROP` applies to the raw term, not automatically to every possible derivative need.

## Trends evidence

Use the repository's rolling weekly history as evidence, not as an automatic scoring system.

Look for:

- whether meaningful activity existed in prior years;
- whether the current rise is first-time, repeated, or seasonal;
- recent slope and acceleration;
- spike → collapse versus rise → persistence;
- earlier comparable peaks;
- pullback followed by a second rise;
- whether the current level is exceptional relative to the prior baseline.

Preserve the API's null semantics. Do not turn missing history into zero.

Do not confuse Trends score with search volume.

## Search-volume evidence

Check Google Ads volume **after** a term passes the opportunity filter.

Use it to understand potential scale, not to decide whether a new demand exists.

A true emerging term may show low or zero historical volume because:

- the term is too new;
- monthly historical metrics lag;
- the query is still fragmenting into multiple phrasings.

Do not replace SEO competition analysis with Google Ads competition.

## Output reasoning standard

Every `KEEP` should answer all of these:

1. **Why now?**
2. **What is the user's repeatable job?**
3. **Why can this become an SEO asset?**
4. **What does Trends history say about lifecycle?**
5. **What real evidence supports the query/need?**

If one of these cannot be answered, downgrade to `WATCH` or `DROP`.

## Common failure modes

- Keeping a movie, match, celebrity, or news term just because volume is large.
- Calling every steep curve a “new keyword.”
- Treating an event window itself as the SEO opportunity.
- Restricting discovery to AI, games, or other familiar categories.
- Inventing “calculator / generator / checker” derivatives without evidence.
- Dropping an entire lead before checking whether the event produced a real derivative need.
- Using search volume as the first filter.
- Using Trends score, Ads competition, or rank as SEO difficulty.
